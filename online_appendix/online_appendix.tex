\section{The earnings process}
\label{oa:process}

\subsection{Residual earnings and the weighting of the minimum-distance fit}

All dynamic estimation in Section~\ref{P-sec:process} of the paper uses residual log earnings: the residual from a pooled regression of log labor income on year fixed effects, a quartic in age, gender and its interactions with the age quartic, urban status, and province fixed effects, winsorized at the first and ninety-ninth percentiles within each wave. Year effects are common rather than group-specific, so between-group differences survive into the residual, where the growth regression of Section~\ref{P-sec:abb} of the paper picks them up; the second-moment estimation additionally demeans within group and year, so that group means do not mechanically enter the autocovariances. The level regressions of Section~\ref{P-sec:premium} of the paper do not use these residuals; they include the controls directly.

The minimum-distance fit weights each autocovariance by the number of pairs of observations behind it. The weighting matrix is fixed a priori rather than estimated, because with 420 children in the smaller group an estimated optimal weighting matrix would introduce the small-sample bias that \citet{AltonjiSegal1996} document. Under fixed weights the minimized criterion is not asymptotically chi-squared (Proposition~\ref{fr:prop:asy}), and the paper reports it as a fit statistic. The comparison between groups rests on the distance test and the scale model of the next sections, not on the criterion values themselves.

\subsection{Separate and joint fits by family background}
\label{oa:jointfit}

Table~\ref{P-tab:md} of the paper fits the permanent--transitory model to each group and, in its last row, to both groups together. \subsection{Diagnostics for the dynamic-panel estimate}
\label{oa:abdiag}

Table~\ref{oatab:abdiag} reports the serial-correlation tests behind
Table~\ref{oatab:ab}. On either panel, the $m_2$ test does not reject the absence of second-order serial correlation, which the instruments dated two waves back require. The five-wave estimates are computed three times: in Python, in a Mata implementation that builds the same moments, and in Stata's \texttt{xtabond2} under the same collapsed instrument layout with Windmeijer-corrected standard errors. Python and Mata agree to four decimals on both coefficients, their standard errors, and the Hansen statistic; \texttt{xtabond2} gives 0.058 (SE 0.036) and 0.010 (SE 0.096) for the two coefficients of Table~\ref{oatab:ab}, and a Hansen statistic of 2.34 against 2.38. For the system GMM row, Python and \texttt{xtabond2} give persistence of 0.109 and 0.111, and the Hansen test rejects in both (20.86 and 20.49 on four degrees of freedom). The Stata files are in \folder{income_dynamics/code/stata}, and \code{income_dynamics/output/tables/stata_abgmm_parity.csv} collects the three sets of estimates.

\subsection{The first stage by family background: details}

Section~\ref{P-sec:firststage} of the paper estimates the first stage separately for the two groups in one regression.

\emph{Completion across cohorts.} Advantaged children complete college at a rate of 17.3 percent in the cohorts born in 1980 or earlier and 50.5 percent in those born later; less advantaged children start at 4.6 percent and reach 24.2 percent. Completion therefore rose by about 33 percentage points among advantaged children and by 20 among less advantaged children, so the gap in percentage points widened from 13 to 26 while the ratio of the two completion rates fell from 3.7 to 2.1. These are changes across cohorts with the national expansion included.

\emph{Province effects common to the groups.} Birth-year effects are specific to each group, so each group's national rise in completion is removed separately. Province effects are common to the groups because the advantaged children, 755 in all and 453 with a level of earnings (fewer than 20 in 23 of the 29 provinces), are too few to estimate a separate set of province effects for them. On all 4,536 children the group first stages are 0.137 (less advantaged) and 0.118 (advantaged), a difference of $-0.019$ with a standard error of 0.019. \input{tables/oa_fsmde} On the rows at 30 and over, which the returns of Section~\ref{P-sec:earnings} of the paper use, the group first stages are 0.182 and 0.189 (Table~\ref{P-tab:groups} of the paper), again not distinguishable from each other; the paper's statement that the added seats raised completion by a similar amount in both groups rests on the estimates on all 4,536 children, which use every cohort.

\emph{The arithmetic of equal additions.} The first stage identifies the allocation of the seats that provinces added beyond the national path, not that of the national expansion itself, which the birth-year effects absorb. An equal addition of $x$ percentage points to starting rates of 17 and 5 percent leaves the gap in percentage points unchanged at 12 and lowers the ratio from $17/5$ to $(17 + x)/(5 + x)$. The similar group first stages therefore imply that the gap in percentage points widened by about as much in provinces that added few seats as in those that added many, while the ratio fell everywhere. The cross-cohort changes of the first paragraph come from the national rise in admissions, in which completion rose by more points among advantaged children.

\subsection{Inference}

Intensity varies by province, so inference rests on 29 clusters. The
$p$-values of the reduced forms are from a wild cluster bootstrap with
the null imposed: the outcome is regressed on the controls alone, the
residuals are multiplied by one Rademacher draw per province, and the cluster-robust $t$-statistic on the instrument is recomputed in each
of 9,999 replications. In the two-row regression the instrument tested is that of the row at 30 and over, and the instrument of the row
below 30 is among the controls. The Anderson--Rubin set for the return to college collects the values $b_0$ at which the same test does not
reject a zero coefficient on that instrument in the regression of
earnings minus $b_0$ times college completion at 30 and over, over a
grid from $-6$ to 6 with step 0.05. As $b_0$ grows without bound the test becomes the test of a zero first stage, so a set is unbounded on a side when the test of a zero first stage does not reject at that level. The bootstrap weights are
drawn once and reused at every point of the grid. The two-row
regression has two endogenous regressors, college completion in each
row, and two instruments, so two-stage least squares and LIML coincide.
In the regression on the rows at 30 and over only, with one instrument
and one endogenous regressor, the cluster-robust first-stage $F$ equals
the effective $F$ of \citet{OleaPflueger2013}.

Table~\ref{oatab:randomization} reports randomization inference, which does not rest on the number of provinces being large. The 29 provincial values of the main intensity measure are reassigned at random among the provinces 4,999 times; the cohort exposure, the outcomes, and the controls of every child stay as they are. For each reassignment the two-row regression is estimated again, and the $p$-value is the share of reassignments, the actual assignment counted among them, whose province-clustered $t$-statistic on the instrument at 30 and over is at least as large in absolute value as the actual one. A second scheme reassigns intensity only among the provinces of the same region (ten provinces in the east, eight in the center, and eleven in the west). Inverting the within-region test over the grid of the Anderson--Rubin sets gives a set for the return to college; the test at each value assumes the same return for every child.

\subsection{Inside the entropy-balanced comparison}

Inside the comparison of advantaged children with reweighted less advantaged children, every term of the identity is a directly measured mean, and the premium of 0.072 splits exactly into three parts (Section~\ref{P-sec:mediation} of the paper). Graduates out-earn children without a degree by 0.541 log points among advantaged children and by 0.517 among reweighted less advantaged children; 33.55 percent of the former and 21.30 percent of the latter hold a degree. The additional degrees of advantaged children, valued at the earnings difference of 0.541 between advantaged graduates and advantaged children without a degree, account for 0.066 (95 percent bootstrap interval $[0.04, 0.09]$); the degrees held in both groups, worth 0.024 more to advantaged children, account for 0.005 ($[-0.02, 0.04]$); and the earnings gap between the two groups among children without a degree is 0.001 ($[-0.08, 0.07]$). The intervals come from 1,000 replications that resample children within group and solve the weights again.
